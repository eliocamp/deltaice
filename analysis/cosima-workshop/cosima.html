Figure 13 shows the correlation of sea ice anomalies for February and September. In summer, all simulations have comparably low correlation values; however, in spring, the CTS-based models show higher correlation than the coupled model at the ice edge, although correlations are near zero in the core of the ice pack. This is likely related to the low variance of sea ice concentrations in that region, where it is almost 100% every September Fig. 14.

While the CTS-based models perform well in terms of correlation and Pan-Antarctic sea ice area, one important limitation is that the variability of the simulated sea ice concentrations is much lower than that of either observations or the coupled model simulation (Figure 14). Lower variability is not unexpected since the Concentration Tendency Surface omits many sources of variability such as advection, divergence, thickness redistribution, and ocean processes. This lack of variability in sea ice concentration does not translate to lower variability in sea ice area, indicating that regional anomalies largely compensate each other when aggregated.

Concentration Tendency Surface in CMIP6 models

The observed Concentration Tendency Surface is an emergent property of thermodynamic processes and interactions between sea ice and the atmosphere. To the extent that climate models represent these correctly, they should simulate a CTS similar to the observed one, thus motivating the use of CTS as a model evaluation framework.

Figure 15 shows the CTS of the selected CMIP6 models computed using the historical simulations. The overall shape of the CTS is similar in all models and consistent with observations, including the dependency of \(T_f\) on sea ice concentrations. In most models, \(T_f\) is colder than observations, meaning that they lose sea ice at lower temperatures. A particularly dramatic example is MIROC6 (Fig. 15 panel s), which loses ice on average at temperatures as cold as -6°C at 50% sea ice concentration. NESM3 is the only model with a significantly warmer \(T_f\) (Fig. 15 panel j). In other models, such as MRI-ESM2.0, CanESM5 and CAS-ESM2.0, the \(T_f\) zero line follows observations remarkably well. Models’ CTSs are stable in time and do not differ substantially between early in historical simulations (Fig. 20), late (Fig. 21) or SSP5-8.5 scenario (Fig. 22).

Beyond \(T_f\), models have slightly different sensitivities in their response to temperature. CAMS-CSM1.0 (Fig. 15 panel o), for example, appears to simulate stronger melt and growth than observed, and the CMCC family of models (Fig. 15 panels g and h) simulates stronger growth. Figure 16 shows the bias and RMSE of each model’s CTS compared with observations. Of the 19 evaluated models, 9 have an overall negative bias and 10 have a positive bias. For ACCESS-ESM1.5, even though its zero line is slightly cold (Fig. 15 panel i) and it stands in the middle of the pack in terms of RMSE, negative and positive errors are compensated, leading to statistically zero bias. The magnitude of positive biases is similar to the magnitude of negative biases, and the average bias across models is -0 (CI: -0.02 – 0.02), indicating that neither warm nor cold models dominate. Furthermore, there does not appear to be a strong relationship between bias and RMSE. MIROC6 is an outlier in that regard, with very large error and negative bias.
